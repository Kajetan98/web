Why a pulse oximeter gets it wrong in microgravity

12 February 2026 Anna Nowak 7 min read

A pulse oximeter is one of the simplest devices on a hospital ward and one of the most treacherous in orbit. The reason isn't the electronics — it's an assumption nobody wrote into the manual.

Saturation measurement relies on the difference in how oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin absorb light. For that difference to be readable, a pulsatile wave of arterial blood with a predictable volume has to pass through the measured site, usually a fingertip. That predictability is exactly what disappears on entering weightlessness.

Fluids shift towards the head

On Earth, gravity keeps part of the blood volume in the lower body. Once that load is removed, fluids move upward — hence the characteristic facial puffiness and blocked nose astronauts describe in the first days of a flight. The limbs lose part of their volume at the same time.

For a finger sensor this means a weaker, shallower pulsatile wave. The signal is still there, but the signal-to-noise ratio drops and the algorithm reads that state as poorer oxygenation. The result is a systematic understatement that is neither a hardware fault nor a patient condition.

Why this is worse than plain inaccuracy

Random measurement error averages out. Systematic error does not. If a device understates the result in the same direction every time, then after a few days of flight the thresholds set on Earth start firing for no reason. And an alert that turns out to be false a dozen times in a row stops being taken seriously by the crew.

The worst case isn't a bad reading. It's a bad reading that everyone has stopped believing.

What we do about it

We don't try to fix the physics of the measurement — we try to give the system an awareness of the conditions it is working in. In practice that comes down to three things:

  • The baseline is captured again after adaptation to weightlessness, not only before launch.
  • Signal quality is reported alongside the result, so a reading from a weak pulse is visibly uncertain.
  • Alert thresholds move with each person's baseline instead of staying at their Earth values.

None of this is spectacular. All of it comes down to the same principle: the device has to say plainly when it stops being confident in its own result. That is the only way anyone is still looking at it several weeks into a mission.
